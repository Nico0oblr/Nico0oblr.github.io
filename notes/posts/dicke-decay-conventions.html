Dicke superradiance as a continuous-time Markov chain

Collective spins
Markov chains
Numerical methods
Collective decay as a pure-death process: population dynamics, exponential waiting times, and exact trajectory sampling.
Published

November 12, 2026

Dicke superradiance provides a useful bridge between open quantum systems and classical stochastic processes. For a state diagonal in the symmetric Dicke basis, collective decay is described exactly by a continuous-time Markov chain. Each emission removes one excitation; the time spent waiting for that emission is random.

For a specified initial excitation number, the sequence of states is fixed. The randomness lies in the waiting times.

From the master equation to transition rates

Consider \(N\) identical two-level emitters with collective lowering operator \(J_- = \sum_{i=1}^N \sigma_-^{(i)}\) and master equation

\[ \dot\rho = \Gamma\,\mathcal D[J_-]\rho, \qquad \mathcal D[L]\rho = L\rho L^\dagger -\frac12\left\{L^\dagger L,\rho\right\}. \]

Here \(\Gamma>0\) is the single-emitter population decay rate. We assume collective decay only, with no drive or other loss channels, and restrict to the fully symmetric sector \(j=N/2\).

Let \(\lvert k\rangle=\lvert j=N/2,m=k-N/2\rangle\) denote the Dicke state with \(k\) excitations. The ladder matrix element is

\[ J_-\lvert k\rangle =\sqrt{k(N-k+1)}\,\lvert k-1\rangle. \]

A diagonal initial state, including the fully excited state, remains diagonal:

\[ \rho(t)=\sum_{k=0}^N p_k(t)\lvert k\rangle\langle k\rvert. \]

Its populations obey

\[ \dot p_k=r_{k+1}p_{k+1}-r_kp_k, \qquad r_k=\Gamma k(N-k+1), \]

where \(r_0=0\) and the inflow term is absent at \(k=N\).

A pure-death Markov chain

Introduce a classical random variable \(K_t\in\{0,1,\ldots,N\}\) for the number of remaining excitations. Its only allowed transition is

\[ k\longrightarrow k-1 \quad\text{at rate}\quad r_k=\Gamma k(N-k+1). \]

This is a pure-death process, with \(k=0\) an absorbing state. The correspondence is

A diagonal mixture corresponds to drawing the initial state \(K_0\) with probabilities \(p_k(0)\).

Using a column probability vector \(\boldsymbol p=(p_0,\ldots,p_N)^\mathsf T\), write

\[ \dot{\boldsymbol p}=Q\boldsymbol p, \qquad Q_{k-1,k}=r_k,\quad Q_{k,k}=-r_k \quad (k=1,\ldots,N), \]

with all remaining entries zero, including \(Q_{0,0}=0\). Each column sums to zero, expressing conservation of probability, and

\[ \boldsymbol p(t)=e^{Qt}\boldsymbol p(0). \]

The quantum model determines the rates. Once these rates are known, the population evolution can be studied using the tools of finite-state Markov chains.

Waiting times and the emission burst

At level \(k>0\), the waiting time \(\tau_k\) until the next photon has survival probability

\[ \Pr(\tau_k>\tau)=e^{-r_k\tau}, \qquad \mathbb E[\tau_k]=\frac{1}{r_k}. \]

After the jump, the process starts a new exponential waiting time at level \(k-1\). The embedded discrete jump chain simply visits \(N,N-1,\ldots,0\) when initialized at full excitation. The holding times are independent exponentials, with a different rate at each level.

The fully excited state has rate \(r_N=\Gamma N\). Around the middle of the ladder, the rate is of order \(\Gamma N^2\), so a trajectory passes through these levels much faster. For comparison, independent emitters give a pure-death process with rates \(r_k=\Gamma k\).

The ensemble photon emission rate is

\[ I(t)=\mathbb E[r_{K_t}] =\sum_{k=0}^N r_kp_k(t) =-\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb E[K_t]. \]

This distinguishes the instantaneous rate at a particular level from the experimentally averaged emission curve, which also depends on the distribution of occupied levels.

Two emitters: equal rates, different populations

For \(N=2\), both nonzero transition rates are equal:

\[ 2\xrightarrow{\,2\Gamma\,}1\xrightarrow{\,2\Gamma\,}0. \]

Starting in \(k=2\), the populations are

\[ p_2(t)=e^{-2\Gamma t},\qquad p_1(t)=2\Gamma t\,e^{-2\Gamma t},\qquad p_0(t)=1-(1+2\Gamma t)e^{-2\Gamma t}. \]

The time of the second photon is the sum of two independent exponential waiting times with rate \(2\Gamma\), an Erlang distribution. The factor of \(t\) in \(p_1\) also illustrates why formulas that assume distinct decay rates need care: Dicke rates satisfy \(r_k=r_{N-k+1}\).

Exact sampling in Julia

A trajectory can be sampled by drawing the waiting time at each level. This is the single-channel version of a Gillespie jump simulation; it introduces no time-step discretization.

using Random

function dicke_chain(N::Integer; Γ = 1.0, rng = Random.default_rng())
    N >= 0 || throw(ArgumentError("N must be nonnegative"))
    Γ > 0 || throw(ArgumentError("Γ must be positive"))

    times = [0.0]
    excitations = [N]
    t = 0.0

    for k in N:-1:1
        rate = Γ * k * (N - k + 1)
        t += randexp(rng) / rate
        push!(times, t)
        push!(excitations, k - 1)
    end

    return (; times, excitations)
end

path = dicke_chain(10; rng = Xoshiro(42))

path.times[2:end] contains the photon emission times. Between successive times, the excitation number is constant; after the last emission it remains zero. Averaging the occupancy of each level over many such paths recovers \(p_k(t)\).

Scope of the correspondence

The classical process describes excitation populations and photon-counting trajectories for the diagonal states considered here. The quantum state contains additional structure: individual Dicke basis states are generally entangled, and the separability of a mixture requires a separate analysis. Coherences and other measurement schemes require their own descriptions.
